## What the numbers show

- Reference points. Chance is 0.005. A frequency prior built from the other 17 templates with all context removed gets 55 of 399 (top-1 0.138); it always proposes the same technique. The same prior that never repeats a seen step gets 71. Both priors use the author-written templates, so they are not 'no history'.
- History of similar but not identical attacks (the other 17 templates, never the incident's own), with context: 129 of 399 (top-1 0.323, tie-aware 0.309, top-3 0.353), +74 against the prior.
- Where the gain over the prior comes from, by the step being predicted: step 2: +37; step 3: -2; step 4: +3; step 5: +12; step 6 or later: +24. It is not concentrated in the entry steps. With every template of the incident's own family removed from the history, the model gets 69 of 399 (+14 against the prior).
- By how similar the nearest other template is (Markov against the prior): 0.70 or more (near sibling): 57 against 28 of 146; 0.50 to 0.70: 18 against 8 of 43; 0.30 to 0.50: 52 against 19 of 171; under 0.30 (unrelated): 2 against 0 of 39. The benefit is absent for unrelated templates but does NOT grow steadily with similarity. For 3 of 18 templates history makes prediction worse than the prior (G12, G13, G16). The templates that reorder another one are among them: the model replays the twin's order.
- Real ordered emulation plans as the only history: 29 of 399 with context, 5 as a context-free prior. They beat chance, but stay below the in-vocabulary template prior (55). Added to the other templates they lower the count from 129 to 116; restricted to the dataset's vocabulary, 50 alone and 122 pooled. Coverage: 26 of the dataset's 29 parent techniques occur in the plans; 39 of the plans' 65 do not occur in the dataset; 61 of 399 test transitions occur as adjacent steps in at least one plan.
- MITRE group technique lists (no order) as history: 8 of 399.
- History that contains the incident's own template: 166 of 399 (top-1 0.416). By instance: exact 54 of 83 (0.651); heavy 46 of 159 (0.289); light 66 of 157 (0.420).
- Second-order context adds nothing for related history: order 1 alone gets 135 against 129.
- Strict end-to-end with the label-free pipeline: real plans: 15 of 399 (0.038); other templates: 78 of 399 (0.195); all templates (own included): 87 of 399 (0.218).

## Caveats

- What E1 supports: a Markov table built from related attacks in the same technique vocabulary predicts the next step better than a context-free prior. What it does not support: that the benefit grows with similarity, or that independently written history (the real emulation plans) helps.
- The templates, their shared prefixes and the similarity between them were all designed by the author of this project. 'Other templates' is a controlled stand-in for 'the history contains related attacks'.
- The eight real plans are emulation plans written from public reporting; their order is the plan's, not telemetry. One (APT29) is two scenarios back to back, one (FIN6) lists alternative end phases one after the other, and three have no initial-access step. They have 18 to 65 steps against 5 to 7 for a template.
- One template is 5 incidents and 15 to 31 prediction instances; a similarity bin can hold as few as two templates. Per-template counts are in the table.
- The model is the same Markov table with back-off in every 'Markov' row; only its training data changes.

## What the numbers show

- Matching, ground-truth trajectories: on the four templates that are reorderings of each other, order finds the right one for 12 of 20 incidents against 0 without order. That holds by construction: the pairs contain the same techniques. On the other 70 incidents order changes nothing (69 without, 69 with).

- Prediction, ground-truth trajectories, history = all templates (own included): top-1 158 of 399 without order, 166 with order (+8). Top-3 0.581 vs 0.589 and MRR 0.538 vs 0.504; with the Markov ranking padded to a comparable length, top-3 0.627 and MRR 0.551.
- Matching, pipeline trajectories: on the four templates that are reorderings of each other, order finds the right one for 11 of 20 incidents against 0 without order. That holds by construction: the pairs contain the same techniques. On the other 70 incidents order changes nothing (41 without, 41 with).
- Prediction, pipeline trajectories, history = other templates: top-1 94 of 333 without order, 100 with order (+6). Top-3 0.459 vs 0.351 and MRR 0.397 vs 0.333; with the Markov ranking padded to a comparable length, top-3 0.432 and MRR 0.412.
- Prediction, pipeline trajectories, history = all templates (own included): top-1 122 of 333 without order, 108 with order (-14). Top-3 0.538 vs 0.447 and MRR 0.481 vs 0.391; with the Markov ranking padded to a comparable length, top-3 0.514 and MRR 0.458.
- Prediction on the four reordered templates only (ground truth, history = other templates): 26 of 85 with order against 24 without. Order gives no clear advantage here: the history contains the twin with the other order, and the order-aware model tends to follow it.

## Caveats

- What E2 supports: order separates two attacks that contain the same techniques, and gives a modest top-1 gain in prediction. What it does not support: a general gain in matching (none outside the reordered pairs) or in ranked prediction.
- Two template pairs (G01/G12 and G04/G13) contain exactly the same techniques in a different order. Without order their references are indistinguishable, so a tie is the best a static match can do. The result on them demonstrates the mechanism; it was built into the dataset.
- The plain Markov ranking lists only continuations seen after the matched context, often one or two, while the static predictor ranks every co-occurring technique. Top-3 and MRR therefore favour the static predictor for a reason unrelated to order; the padded row removes most of that difference.
- 'Static' and 'temporal' are two scoring rules over the same data: set overlap vs overlap plus edit distance for matching; co-occurrence vs Markov transitions for prediction.
- On pipeline trajectories the prediction target is the pipeline's own next step, mapping errors included; those rows compare the two rules on the same noisy input and are not end-to-end accuracy.

## What the numbers show

- ground-truth trajectories: 60 of 78 classified correctly with the order-aware score (majority-class baseline 30; 65 with thresholds fitted on all incidents); 38 with the order-free score. 11 of 30 novel incidents were taken for a known or variant attack; near-novel recognised as novel 4 / 15, far-novel 15 / 15. AUC known vs variant 0.948; AUC (known + variant) vs novel 0.872.
- pipeline trajectories: 30 of 78 classified correctly with the order-aware score (majority-class baseline 30; 38 with thresholds fitted on all incidents); 40 with the order-free score. 12 of 30 novel incidents were taken for a known or variant attack; near-novel recognised as novel 3 / 15, far-novel 15 / 15. AUC known vs variant 0.594; AUC (known + variant) vs novel 0.638. Of the pipeline's incidents with no attack in them, 6 / 87 would be called a known or variant attack.
- Sensitivity to which six templates are held out (ground truth, 100 random sets): order-aware 51 to 76 of 78 correct (median 66); order-free 31 to 66 (median 50). The order-aware score was ahead in 100 of 100 sets. The set used here gives 60 and 38.

## Caveats

- What E3 supports: unrelated attacks are recognised as novel; the order-aware score is better than the order-free one across held-out choices. What it does not support: telling a near relative of a known attack from a variant of it, or anything on the label-free pipeline.
- Known versus variant is close to arithmetic. One edit to a 5-, 6- or 7-step template gives a similarity of 0.800 to 0.875 to its own reference by the definition of the score; two edits give less. The classifier is largely recovering the number of edits. Only the novel boundary is informative.
- Classes: known = an instance with ONE edit of a template that is in the history; variant = TWO edits; novel = any instance of the six templates left out of the history. The 12 exact (verbatim) instances of in-history templates are not classified; their scores are listed separately. This removes the tautology of the first E3, where 'known' meant a verbatim copy.
- Three of the six novel templates were chosen to be near relatives of in-history ones (G02 extends G01, G08 shares the phishing entry of G05 and the ending of G01, G13 reorders G04) and three to be unrelated. A system that calls a near relative 'variant' is arguably right; the near/far split shows where the novel errors are.
- The known / variant boundary is one edit vs two on sequences of 5 to 7 steps, a narrow margin by design.
- Thresholds are chosen by leave-one-out; the in-sample count shows how much they move.
- Which six templates are held out was fixed before any result was computed and not varied.
